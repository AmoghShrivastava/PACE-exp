How our table-tennis robot learned to return serves

Every part of how the final robot was trained: what it senses, what it controls, how it is scored, how it practises and improves, and how the human motion capture was used. Each chapter gives the idea in plain words, then the exact technical detail, then why it was done that way.

Chapter 1

The goal

A serving machine on the far side of the table fires balls at a humanoid robot. The robot must walk to the right spot, swing, hit the ball, and land it on the opponent's half. The ball takes under half a second to arrive, so footwork and stroke have to happen together.

PACE (Purdue, ICRA 2026) learns this with one neural network that controls the whole body. We rebuilt PACE on HIM Arena compute, fixed what kept it from training well, and added a reward built from HIM Arena's human motion capture.

Chapter 2

Learning by trial and error

The robot is controlled by a policy, a neural network mapping what it senses (the observation) to what each joint should do (the action). It starts random, practises in simulation, and receives a reward every step. Training adjusts the network so actions that led to more total reward become more likely. This is reinforcement learning (RL).

Like learning a sport blindfolded from a coach who only says "good" or "bad", but with billions of attempts.

Under the hood

Formally the task is a partially observed Markov decision process. The policy maximizes the expected discounted return E[Σ γᵗ rₜ] with γ = 0.95. The actor outputs a Gaussian over actions (mean from the network, one learned standard deviation shared across joints, initialized at 1.0); during testing the mean is used.

Why

Hand-writing a controller that balances, steps and swings a 23-joint humanoid within 0.5 s is impractical. RL discovers coordinated whole-body motion by optimization.

Chapter 3

Robot and simulator

  • Robot: Booster T1, 1.2 m, 23 joints. 21 are actuated by the policy (6 per leg, 4 per arm, 1 waist); 2 head joints are fixed. A paddle is mounted on the right hand; its contact point is 0.345 m along the hand link.
  • Simulator: Isaac Sim 4.5 + Isaac Lab 2.2, PhysX on GPU, on one NVIDIA A40 (48 GB) rented through HIM Arena.
  • Timing: physics step 2 ms; the policy acts every 10 physics steps, so it runs at 50 Hz.
  • Ball: 40 mm, 3.4 g, restitution 0.9, quadratic air drag (drag coefficient 0.4378, fitted by the PACE authors to real flights).
  • Scale: 4,096 robots, each at its own table, simulated in parallel.
Our finding

PACE's config includes an optional 5 ms × 4 physics setting for faster runs. With it, our first robots learned about half as fast (training hit rate 2.4% vs 4.9% at iteration 500). A controlled test showed ball–paddle collisions behave the same at both step sizes, so the slowdown is in learning dynamics (the joint PD loop runs at 200 Hz instead of 500 Hz). Everything below uses 2 ms.

Why noise and delay

Real sensors are noisy and late. Training with uniform noise and a 2–5 physics-step perception delay makes the policy robust instead of dependent on perfect data it will never get on hardware.

Chapter 5

What the robot controls

The actor outputs 21 numbers. Each becomes a joint target around a nominal pose, and a PD controller (a stiff virtual spring with damping) drives the joint toward it at 500 Hz.

q_target = q_default + 0.25 · a (radians, a clipped at ±100) τ = K_p (q_target − q) − K_d q̇ (per joint, from the T1 actuator config)

Why position targets

Targets around a stable stance are far easier to learn than raw torques, and the 0.25 scale keeps early random actions small. The nominal pose holds the right arm raised, ready to swing.

Chapter 6

Predicting the ball

The robot has to start moving before the ball arrives, so PACE gives it a forecast of the ideal hitting point: the top of the ball's bounce on the robot's side.

Physics forecast ("oracle")

Uses the simulator's true ball state and closed-form drag physics. Used for rewards and the critic only; it is not available on a real robot.

Learned forecast

An MLP (15 → 64 → 64 → 3) reads the last 5 ball positions and is trained to imitate the oracle (MSE, Adam, lr 5e-4, batch 1,024) during the first 20 iterations, then frozen. About 3 cm error. This is what the actor sees.

Under the hood: the oracle

With quadratic drag constant k = ½ρCdA/m ≈ 0.134 m⁻¹, table height h = 0.78 m and g = 9.81:

time to bounce t₁ = (v_z + √(v_z² + 2g(z − h))) / g vertical after bounce v_z' = −0.9 (v_z − g t₁) time to apex t₂ = atan(v_z'·√(k/g)) / √(gk) apex height z* = h + ln(1 + k v_z'²/g) / (2k) horizontal travel s(T) = sign(v) · ln(1 + k|v|T) / k (0.7 × speed after the bounce)

The predicted apex (x*, y*, z*) is the hitting point; x is clamped to ≤ −1.6 m so the target stays reachable. A target body position (x* − 0.1, y* + 0.6, 0.69 m) and a target body velocity 4 × (target − position), capped at 7 m/s, are derived from it. All three feed the reaching rewards.

Why

PACE's ablation shows that without the forecast the robot essentially never learns to hit. It turns a reactive problem into "get to this point by this time".

Why dense hints

Why randomize

Variety forces a general skill instead of memorizing one serve, and is the main tool for transferring from simulation to a real robot. Desynchronized episodes keep each batch from being dominated by robots all at the same phase.

Chapter 9

The critic

Training uses two networks with the same shape (512 → 512 → 128, ELU). The actor plays. The critic estimates the value V(s), the return expected from the current state, and is used only during training. Because it is discarded afterwards, it is allowed to see privileged information.

Why an asymmetric actor-critic

A critic that knows the true future makes advantage estimates (Chapter 10) far less noisy, so learning is faster and steadier, while the actor still depends only on information a real robot has.

Chapter 10

PPO, step by step

  1. Collect: all 4,096 robots act for 24 steps (0.48 s): 98,304 transitions.
  2. Advantages: with generalized advantage estimation (λ = 0.95), compute how much better each action turned out than the critic expected.
  3. Update: 5 passes over the data in 4 mini-batches of 24,576, maximizing the clipped PPO objective while fitting the critic.
  4. Adapt: if the policy moved too far (KL divergence above 0.01), shrink the learning rate; if too little, grow it.
Under the hood

δₜ = rₜ + γ V(sₜ₊₁) − V(sₜ) γ = 0.95 Âₜ = Σₗ (γλ)ˡ δₜ₊ₗ λ = 0.95 ρₜ = π_new(aₜ|oₜ) / π_old(aₜ|oₜ) L = −E[min(ρₜÂₜ, clip(ρₜ, 0.8, 1.2)Âₜ)] + 1.0·L_value − 0.006·entropy

Value loss is clipped too; gradients are clipped to norm 1.0; learning rate starts at 1e−3 and adapts to a KL target of 0.01. γ = 0.95 means the robot cares about roughly the next 20 steps (0.4 s), the time scale of one stroke.

Why PPO

It is the standard for massively parallel robot RL: on-policy, stable, and simple. The clip keeps each update small so the policy doesn't collapse.

Chapter 11

Human motion pipeline

HIM Arena recorded about 33 minutes of a right-handed player in a Rokoko motion-capture suit. We converted it into robot strokes.

61 bones, 30 fps"] --> B["Blender
global joint poses"]
  B --> G["GMR / mink IK
Booster T1 poses"]
  G --> C["Stroke cutting
1,040 forehands"]
  C --> V["Conditional VAE
+3,404 strokes"]
  V --> L["Library
4,444 × 54 frames × 9 joints"]
Under the hood
  • Export: headless Blender reads each FBX and records every bone's world position and rotation per frame. Frame 0 is a T-pose.
  • Convention change: GMR expects SMPL-X–style rotations. Each bone's rotation is expressed relative to its T-pose and composed with SMPL-X's rest rotation: R'ₜ = Rₜ R₀⁻¹ · Rx(90°).
  • Retargeting: GMR solves inverse kinematics (mink, DAQP solver) on the T1 model in two passes per frame. It matches pelvis, spine, hips, knees, feet, shoulders and the paddle-hand wrist (position weight 20 then 40), with body lengths scaled 0.6 (legs, torso) and 0.7 (arms) for a 1.75 m human.
  • Stroke detection: right-hand speed from a Savitzky–Golay derivative (window 7, order 2); peaks above 1.5 m/s with prominence 0.8, at least 0.4 s apart; forehand side only; frames with high IK error discarded.
  • Windows: −0.54 s to +0.52 s around each peak, resampled to 50 Hz: 54 frames of 9 upper-body joints (right shoulder pitch and roll, right elbow pitch and yaw, waist, left arm 4).
  • Contact key: forward kinematics gives the paddle point at the peak frame, expressed relative to the torso in the torso's heading frame. Mean key ≈ (0.41, −0.32, −0.11) m: forward, to the right, slightly below the chest.
Chapter 12

The motion VAE

The human mostly hit low, but the short robot often needs higher contact points. A conditional variational autoencoder learned the style of the real strokes and generated new ones for chosen contact points (the SMASH paper's augmentation idea).

Under the hood
  • Condition c: the contact key (3) plus the stroke's first frame (9), normalized per joint.
  • Encoder: [54×9 trajectory, c] → 256 → 256 → (μ, log σ²) of a 16-dimensional latent z. Decoder: [z, c] → 256 → 256 → 54×9. ELU, dropout 0.1.
  • Loss: reconstruction MSE + β·KL + 10 × mismatch in second differences (smoothness). β follows a cyclic schedule (4 cycles up to 0.01) to avoid posterior collapse. AdamW, lr 1e−3, weight decay 1e−3, 20,000 steps of batch 256.
  • Generation: 15,000 samples with z ~ N(0, I) and contact keys spread 1.5× wider than the real ones plus noise.
  • Filtering: joint limits (±0.15 rad tolerance, then clipped); jerk no worse than 1.5× the real data's 99th percentile; forward-kinematics contact point within 15 cm of the requested one. 3,404 survived, each keyed by its actual FK contact point.
Why

Motion matching only helps if the library covers the contact points the robot needs. With the VAE strokes, the robot's real contact points were 2–4 cm from a library stroke instead of 8–12 cm.

Chapter 13

The stroke bonus

The human motion enters training as one extra reward term (plus 19 critic inputs). Rewards, actor observations and actions are otherwise PACE's.

1. Match

Each step, the oracle hitting point is converted into the torso-heading frame and compared with all 4,444 library keys (Euclidean distance on GPU); the nearest stroke is selected. This repeats every step while the ball is incoming, so the choice tracks the forecast.

2. Time

frame = clamp(27 − round(t_apex / 0.02), 0, 27) before contact frame = 27, 28, …, 53 after a real paddle hit

3. Score

r_stroke = exp( −Σⱼ wⱼ (qⱼ − q_ref,ⱼ)² / 0.6² ) · gate weight 3.0

JointwⱼReason
Right shoulder pitch, roll; right elbow pitch1.0The stroke
Waist0.5Trunk rotation
Left shoulder pitch, roll; left elbow pitch0.3Balance arm
Both elbow-yaw (forearm twist)0Free to aim the paddle face (SMASH excludes the wrist the same way)
Legs, base pose—Not tracked; footwork is PACE's

4. Gate

gate = 1 if −0.3 s ≤ τ ≤ 0 and no hit yet (the swing) gate = 2 if 0 < τ ≤ 0.2 s after a real hit (follow-through) gate = 0 otherwise τ = time relative to contact

5. Critic-only reference

The critic receives (q_ref − q) at the current frame and 5 frames (0.1 s) ahead, plus the active flag. The actor's 405 inputs are unchanged.

Why each piece
  • Matching and timing make the reference the right stroke at the right moment.
  • Gating closes a loophole found with the always-on version, where the robot farmed style points instead of hitting.
  • Weight 3 was chosen to sit next to PACE's dense hints (2–5) and far below a hit (150), so it shapes rather than overrides.
  • Critic-only keeps the deployed controller identical in what it needs to PACE's.

How much the human motion actually counted

Why so small
  • Short window: the gate is open for only about 0.5 s of each serve, roughly a quarter of the time.
  • Steep exponential: with σ = 0.6 rad and several weighted joints, a stroke that differs by about 0.3 rad per joint already scores only around a third, and larger differences score near zero. Once the robot's own swing diverged from the human style, the bonus fell close to zero.
  • Competition: hitting and returning are worth hundreds of times more, so the optimizer has no reason to give up any hitting to match the human.
What this means

In this configuration the human motion acted as a light early nudge (the swing at iteration 1,000 was 16% closer to human strokes for both stroke-bonus seeds), not as a major training signal. That is why the robot plays as well as plain PACE, but not better, and why the styles converge later. The earlier always-on version had the opposite problem: its stroke reward grew to over 10× the contact reward and pulled the robot away from hitting. To make human motion a strong signal, the next steps would be a larger weight (on the order of 30–100), a wider gate, or a learned style reward (an AMP-style discriminator).

The training run

  • Budget: 10,000 iterations × 98,304 transitions ≈ 983 million decisions ≈ 228 days of play.
  • Compute: one A40 per run via HIM Arena, about 8–11 iterations per minute alone on the GPU; about 17 GPU hours in total.
  • Schedule: three stretches (0–3,500, 3,500–7,800, 7,800–10,000), each resumed from the last checkpoint; seed 42. After each resume, the logged hit rate dips briefly because every robot restarts at once, then recovers within about 100 iterations.

Chapter 17

Testing

Final testing uses PACE's own eval.py, unmodified: fixed start pose, sensor noise on, pushes off, the first 2 × (number of robots) serves skipped as warm-up. A hit is the paddle-contact event; a return is the ball landing on the opponent's half after a paddle touch. The paper's four serve categories were run with as many serves as the paper used.

Reading these fairly

Scoring is PACE's own. Our robot runs in our subclass of PACE's environment (its critic used the stroke reference), the single-category serve ranges were added as configs from the paper's Table II, and it is one trained robot, so seed-to-seed spread is unmeasured. A controlled test of PACE with and without the stroke bonus shows the performance gain comes from our PACE build, not from the human motion (Chapter 14).

Chapter 18

Glossary

Actor / policy
The network that controls the robot: 405 observation numbers in, 21 joint offsets out.
Critic / value function
A training-only network estimating expected future reward from privileged state.
Advantage
How much better an action turned out than the critic expected; positive means "do more of this".
PPO
Proximal Policy Optimization: improves the policy in small clipped steps.
GAE
Generalized advantage estimation: blends short- and long-horizon advantage estimates (λ = 0.95).
Iteration
One collect-and-update cycle: 4,096 robots × 24 steps, then 5 epochs of updates.
Oracle
The exact physics forecast of the ball, available only in simulation.
Domain randomization
Varying physics, serves, noise and delays during training so the skill generalizes.
Retargeting
Inverse kinematics that finds the robot pose closest to a human pose.
Motion matching
Picking the library motion whose key (here, the contact point) is closest to the current need.
VAE
Variational autoencoder: learns a compact latent space of examples and samples new ones from it.
AMP
Adversarial Motion Priors: a style reward from a discriminator trained to tell robot motion from human motion.
Seed
The starting value for all randomness; same seed and settings give identical training here.
